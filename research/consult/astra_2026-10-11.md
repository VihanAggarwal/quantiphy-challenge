## Bottom line

I would **not** spend the remaining budget on another general-purpose answer ensemble, better bounding boxes, or a broader agent loop.

## First, use the correct leverage weights

Because categories are equally weighted, raw question counts are misleading.

| Subset | Fraction of total MRA weight |
|---|---:|
| D2 static targets: size + distance | 19.4% |
| D3 static targets: size + distance | 19.4% |

Thus:

- Improving all lab questions by 0.10 gives **+0.0266 overall**.
- A segmentation improvement also benefits originals if you transfer **measurements**, not just answers.

This strongly favors shared calibration fixes over individual-answer polishing.

## My ranking of likely error sources

### A. Shared scale errors from dynamic priors: highest priority

The pattern “D2 motion .860, static .791” is particularly informative.

With a motion prior, some errors cancel when predicting another motion quantity. They do **not** cancel when predicting a size. A noisy acceleration estimate can therefore corrupt every size answer while leaving certain motion ratios tolerable.

For planar motion:

\[
s=\frac{v_{\rm prior}}{\|\dot u_{\rm prior}\|},
\qquad
s=\frac{a_{\rm prior}}{\|\ddot u_{\rm prior}\|},
\qquad
L=s\,w_{\rm pixels}.
\]

For acceleration priors, all of the following become shared multiplicative errors:

- Tracking the wrong reference point.
- Differentiating a rotating or articulated feature.
- Fitting through a collision or acceleration change.
- Using the wrong time convention.
- Confusing tangential acceleration with acceleration-vector magnitude.
- Treating camera motion as object motion.

Your failed optical-flow experiment does **not** rule this out. It rules out that particular flow estimator and refinement scheme.

### B. 3D model mismatch and missing observability: also high priority

“Geometry is worse than direct” does not imply that geometry is intrinsically unhelpful. It suggests that the implemented geometric model is often wrong or underconstrained.

Likely culprits include:

- Camera distance versus optical-axis depth.
- Unknown focal length.
- Object orientation and foreshortening.
- Treating center depth as the depth of both dimension endpoints.
- Ignoring radial motion when converting image motion to speed.
- Measuring screen width when the question asks a canonical object dimension.

These are **structural errors**, not pixel-precision errors. Better masks cannot repair them.

### C. Motion estimation and event timing: high priority for motion targets

Sixteen uniform frames can be enough for constant velocity. It is often inadequate for:

- Acceleration near a specified time.
- A bounce or collision.
- Short free-flight segments.
- A turning point.
- A brief motion onset.
- Trimmed clips whose queried time is outside the observed interval.

Adding the frame at the queried time does not solve this: derivatives require an appropriate neighborhood around that time.

### D. Object/dimension/reference-point mistakes: probably a meaningful tail

I would expect a minority of videos to contain large semantic errors:

- Width versus length of an oblique car/book.
- Whole rider versus scooter chassis.
- Ball center versus changing bounding-box center.
- Wrong similarly colored instance.
- “Average speed” interpreted as displacement divided by duration.
- A family transfer between nominally similar but distinct objects.

These errors are expensive under MRA and are worth targeted adjudication. They do not justify rerunning every video.

### E. Ordinary extent precision: lower priority

Your own crop experiment is strong evidence: substantially better extents modeled only **+0.003 MRA**.

That does **not** mean motion precision is unimportant. Second derivatives are vastly more sensitive than widths.

### F. Label noise and ambiguity: real, but not an explanation for all the gap

Twelve suspect validation items out of 159 is roughly 7.5%, but neither the prevalence nor irreducibility is established.

As a conditional illustration:

- If 7.5% of items were unavoidable zeroes, the ceiling would be about **0.925**.
- If those items remained partially scoreable, the ceiling would be higher.
- Unobservable dimensions, clipped events, and ambiguous conventions could lower it further.

I would not claim a data-supported ceiling more precise than that. **0.85–0.88 looks like a credible engineering objective; 0.90 requires several substantial fixes to work together.**

# 2. Before building anything: three diagnostic oracles on validation

These are diagnostics, **not performance estimates**.

### Oracle A: best scalar correction per video

Find the best multiplicative correction to all existing answers in each validation video.

Report how much loss this removes, separately for:

- Static targets.
- Motion targets.
- Speed-prior videos.
- Acceleration-prior videos.

If one scalar removes a large fraction of the error, prioritize calibration. If it barely helps, prioritize semantics and projection.

Also inspect within-video log residuals:

\[
r_i=\log(\hat y_i/y_i).
\]

A common offset across questions indicates scale. A dependence on derivative order indicates timing or kinematics.

### Oracle B: best choice between current geometry and direct answers

Compute the validation oracle that chooses the better existing answer for each question.

This gives an upper bound on the value of better routing. If the oracle gain is tiny, do not spend days designing a router.

### Oracle C: manually verified specifications, without remeasuring pixels

For a small sample, correct only:

- Object identity.
- Canonical dimension.
- Prior semantics.
- Time/event interval.
- Reference point.

Then rerun the existing solver. This separates semantic failures from measurement failures cheaply.

Use video/family-grouped comparisons throughout. You have closer to **24 independent validation units than 159**.

## 1. Audit invariants, especially timing and depth semantics

### What to implement

Create automated tests for the following.

**Resolution invariance**

Running geometry on coordinates at 1280 px and on consistently rescaled coordinates at 854 px must produce identical answers.

For example, a focal length of 722 px at width 1280 becomes approximately **482 px at width 854**, assuming uniform scaling without padding.

Check:

- Long-edge resizing.
- Letterboxing/cropping offsets.
- Normalized versus pixel coordinates.
- Frame-specific crop transforms.
- Whether depth and focal estimates use the same coordinate system.

**Dimensional invariance**

If all length-bearing inputs are multiplied by \(\lambda\)—including depths and the numerical prior, with times unchanged—every requested length, speed, or acceleration should also multiply by \(\lambda\).

This is an excellent solver test. On a few direct-answer calls, it also tests whether the model actually respects the prior.

**Time-order invariance**

If estimated time is \(\beta\) times physical time, a geometry answer calibrated by a prior of derivative order \(k_0\) and predicting order \(k\) scales as:

\[
\hat y/y=\beta^{k_0-k}.
\]

Here size is order 0, speed 1, acceleration 2.

This explains why a timing error can hurt sizes under acceleration priors much more than same-order motion ratios.

### Important: do not blindly apply a 25/24 correction

Dropping frames from 25 fps to 24 fps while preserving duration is **not** a uniform 25/24 speed change.

Instead, consecutive retained frames can span one or two original capture intervals. Recovering that cadence can matter for local derivatives. A blanket correction can make things worse.

Use:

- Actual decoded PTS.
- Motion residuals across several objects.
- Family event alignment.
- A small set of explicit timing hypotheses.

**Depth convention**

Establish whether `depth_info` means optical-axis depth \(Z\) or Euclidean camera distance \(r\). They differ substantially for wide-angle/off-axis objects.

This should be settled before investing in a new 3D solver.

## 2. Use segmented twins as sensors, not answer aliases

Your current “segmented copy takes original answer” throws away their main value.

### Concrete replacement

For each aligned pair:

1. Extract foreground support from the white-background clip.
2. Associate components/regions with cached object specifications.
3. Track object masks, contours, and interior features.
4. Transfer those measurements to the original timeline.
5. Solve both sets of questions from the shared measurement record.

The primary payoff is not another 2% improvement in width. It is:

- Stable object identity.
- Better motion reference points.
- Dense trajectories.
- Occlusion detection.
- Rotation/translation separation.
- Finding the correct event interval.

Pilot 10–15 pairs spanning:

- Acceleration priors.
- Rotating movers.
- Overlap.
- Static dimensions.
- 3D views.

Evaluate:

- Predicting withheld frames.
- Agreement between independent feature subsets.
- Prediction of another family member’s exact prior, withheld from fitting.

A low mask error alone is not enough to proceed.

## 3. Build a rigid-aware dynamic-prior calibrator

This is my largest expected-gain intervention.

### Do not merely replace optical flow with CoTracker

Use CoTracker as a source of observations, then add a measurement model.

For each prior object:

1. Track multiple points on a mechanically appropriate component.
2. Reject points with occlusion, background attachment, or incompatible motion.
3. Fit translation plus rotation/scale as appropriate.
4. Propagate a canonical object reference point through that transform.
5. Fit motion separately within event-consistent intervals.
6. Estimate uncertainty by resampling feature groups and temporal blocks.

Examples:

- Scooter: chassis/wheel axle, not rider’s torso.
- Bike: rigid frame reference, not a tire feature.
- Rolling ball: center, not surface texture.
- Rotating book: transformed canonical center, not changing box center.

### Fit several restricted models

Use a small, predefined library:

- Constant velocity.
- Constant acceleration.
- Smooth local polynomial.
- Piecewise models with a detected impact/onset.
- Projectile motion where justified.

Choose with held-out trajectory prediction and residual structure—not only training residual.

**Average-speed priors** require path length over the specified interval, not automatically net displacement.

**Acceleration priors** require distinguishing:

\[
\|\ddot{\mathbf x}\|
\quad\text{from}\quad
\frac{d\|\dot{\mathbf x}\|}{dt}.
\]

These differ on curved trajectories.

### Why acceleration is especially dangerous

Under ideal independent position noise, a quadratic fit over duration \(T\), with \(N\) observations and pixel noise \(\sigma_p\), has approximately:

\[
\sigma_a \approx
\frac{\sqrt{720}\,\sigma_p}{\sqrt N\,T^2}.
\]

With \(\sigma_p=2\) px, \(N=25\), \(T=1\) s:

\[
\sigma_a\approx 10.7\ {\rm px/s^2}.
\]

If the true projected acceleration is 100 px/s², that is already roughly **11% scale uncertainty**, before width, perspective, or semantics errors.

Doubling a genuinely constant-acceleration interval can help dramatically. Extending across a collision can instead create a confidently wrong estimate.

Correlated tracking errors make the ideal formula optimistic; use block bootstrap estimates.

### Cheap validation and a quantitative payoff model

Pilot 20–30 dynamic-prior clips, emphasizing static targets and high disagreement among existing answers.

Require stability across:

- Two independent feature sets.
- Nearby fitting windows.
- Original versus segmented input.
- Corresponding family views where available.

As a leverage calculation:

\[
0.388 \times 0.40 \times 0.12 \approx 0.019.
\]

That is **+0.019 overall** if you successfully improve 40% of D2/D3 static-target weight by 0.12 per question. That is ambitious but much more plausible than getting +0.02 from better box edges.

## 4. Fit lab trajectories jointly in 3D

“Lab 3D → direct for motion” is the routing choice I would challenge hardest.

You already have unusually valuable information:

- Fixed camera.
- Approximately known focal length.
- Depth information.
- Repeated events/views.
- Many gravity priors.

### Fit image observations directly

For a ballistic segment:

\[
\mathbf P(\tau)
=
\mathbf P_0+\mathbf V_0\tau+\tfrac12\mathbf g\tau^2,
\qquad \|\mathbf g\|=9.8.
\]

Project this trajectory through the camera and jointly fit:

- Initial position and velocity.
- Gravity direction in camera coordinates, if not already known.
- Event start/time offset.
- Any permitted camera/depth nuisance parameters.

Depth observations constrain the fit at their actual timestamps.

Differentiate the fitted **world trajectory**, not the image trajectory.

### Critical gates

Do not impose gravity as the object’s net acceleration when the object is:

- In contact with a surface.
- Driven by a motor or spring.
- Undergoing impact.
- Subject to appreciable drag over the fitted interval.

Likewise, “acceleration at 1.0 s = 2.8” does not establish constant acceleration throughout the clip.

Use gravity as a constraint only on identified free-flight intervals. Use rolling constraints only after testing no-slip; simulated wheel animation may not be physically coupled.

### Cheap validation

Before looking at answer scores, ask:

- Does the fit predict held-out image positions?
- Does it predict withheld depth observations?
- Do independent views recover the same speed magnitude?
- Do background-replaced copies produce the same physical parameters?
- Does a model fitted before a queried time predict its neighborhood correctly?

For queries beyond the clip, extrapolate only when the supported motion model makes it defensible. Otherwise expose the uncertainty rather than issuing a precise quadratic extrapolation.

## 5. Optimize the actual MRA decision, not the median

This is cheap, but it needs a credible uncertainty distribution.

For positive ground truth \(Y\), let

\[
D=\{.05,.1,.2,\ldots,.9\}.
\]

For prediction \(p\),

\[
\mathbb E[\mathrm{MRA}(p,Y)]
=
\frac1{10}\sum_{\delta\in D}
\left[
F\!\left(\frac{p}{1-\delta}\right)
-
F\!\left(\frac{p}{1+\delta}\right)
\right],
\]

where \(F\) is the predictive CDF.

Optimize this one-dimensional objective over candidate predictions.

### Where the distribution should come from

Use uncertainty over:

- Tracks and fitting windows.
- Focal length/depth.
- Competing physical models.
- Plausible dimension interpretations.

Three correlated Claude outputs are **not** a calibrated posterior over truth.

This can also support routing: choose geometry when its assumptions are verified and its predictive interval is narrow; otherwise retain direct or a mixture.

Do not blanket-shrink all answers. The metric’s asymmetry makes some downward adjustments rational under uncertainty, but the optimal adjustment depends on the distribution.

### Cheap validation

Use only a very small number of global calibration choices. Check whether gains survive multiplying uncertainty widths by 0.5 and 2. If the gain exists only at one finely tuned width, discard it.

## 6. Recover 3D calibration only where it is identifiable

I would not retry your previous general 3D geometry substitution. I would build a narrow, explicitly gated branch.

Suppose depth is optical-axis depth and the camera is fixed. Define:

\[
\mathbf q(t)=Z(t)\big(u(t)-c_x,\ v(t)-c_y\big).
\]

Then:

\[
\mathbf P(t)=\left(\frac{\mathbf q(t)}f,\ Z(t)\right).
\]

A speed prior gives:

\[
v^2
=
\frac{\|\dot{\mathbf q}\|^2}{f^2}+\dot Z^2,
\]

so:

\[
f=
\frac{\|\dot{\mathbf q}\|}
{\sqrt{v^2-\dot Z^2}}.
\]

An acceleration-magnitude prior gives the analogous expression using second derivatives.

### Why this could help

It uses the exact dynamic prior to estimate focal length instead of asking the model to guess it.

### Why it must be gated

It fails or becomes ill-conditioned when:

- Depth samples do not constrain the required derivatives.
- Motion is almost entirely radial.
- Camera motion is unmodeled.
- The tracked point does not represent the prior quantity.
- `depth_info` is range, not axial depth.
- The prior is average speed rather than instantaneous speed.

If \(v^2-\dot Z^2\) is small, focal length is poorly identified. That is an abstention signal, not a reason to clamp the denominator.

Even a well-estimated focal length does not identify the canonical width of a heavily foreshortened object. Use frontoparallel dimensions, known shape models, or multi-view evidence.

### Cheap validation

Hold out a family member’s exact size/speed fact and predict it using this calibration. Compare with current direct answers.

Accept this branch only on the observability regime where it wins. Your previous −0.09 to −0.13 result makes a global replacement unjustified.

## 8. Upgrade family pooling into joint physical estimation

You already pool facts and answers. The untried extension is to pool **latent physical parameters and observations**.

A family model could contain:

- Shared object dimensions.
- Shared world trajectories.
- Per-camera intrinsics/extrinsics.
- Per-clip event time offsets.
- Verified exact-prior constraints.
- Image-point, mask, and depth observations.

Fit the family jointly.

The advantage over answer pooling is that one view can resolve another view’s degeneracy:

- Side view constrains length.
- Another view constrains depth or orientation.
- One prior fixes metric scale.
- Another constrains motion.
- A segmented twin improves visibility.

### Start small

Do not build arbitrary differentiable scene reconstruction.

Start with high-yield families containing:

- Several exact anchors.
- Rigid objects.
- Simple shapes: boxes, spheres, cylinders.
- Clear shared events.
- Enough overlapping observations.

### Important safeguards

- Segmented twins are not independent camera views.
- Background replacements may not add geometric information.
- “Time 1 s” in two trimmed clips need not refer to the same event time.
- Similar-looking instances are not necessarily identical props.
- Canonical width and screen width must not be merged.

### Cheap validation

Remove one exact anchor and predict it from the rest. Ensure the remaining graph still has a metric-scale anchor; otherwise the test is underdetermined.

This is more informative than checking that a joint fit reproduces priors it was explicitly forced to satisfy.

# 4. Pipeline choices that look mistaken or at least risky

## A. Hard routing by broad category

“3D non-lab → direct” and “lab motion → direct” discard information about observability.

Routing should depend on:

- Geometry assumptions.
- Track quality.
- Time coverage.
- Depth coverage.
- Projection conditioning.
- Whether the target dimension is visible.

That does not mean immediately replacing the current router. First measure the validation oracle opportunity.

## B. Answer-level twin copying

This is a useful consistency rule but a poor final architecture. The segmented rendering should often be the **better measurement channel**, even if the original is the better semantic channel.

## C. Treating all pixel tracks as interchangeable

A track of a rotating feature, an articulated point, a box center, and a rigid-body reference point are different physical observables.

Your solver needs to know which it has.

## D. Fitting derivatives without explicit event segmentation

A smooth fit through a bounce can have low average pixel error and a completely wrong instantaneous acceleration.

## E. Unverified family transfers

Exact transfers are excellent when identity, quantity, axis, and event time match. Incorrect “exact” transfers can systematically overwrite correct answers.

Audit these before adding more pooling.

## F. Confidence from repeatability

Repeated direct answers or stable polynomial fits can be consistently wrong. Include structural uncertainty, not just run-to-run variation.

## G. Spending uploads to recover hidden-subset information

I would stop further zero-out probing and avoid any attempt at per-item score recovery. Use ordinary aggregate model comparisons within organizer policy. Apart from the rules concern, your remaining uploads are more valuable for testing actual improvements.
